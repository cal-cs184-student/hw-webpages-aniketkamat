Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection

Walk through the ray generation and primitive intersection parts of the rendering pipeline.
The rendering pipeline involved both ray generation and primitive intersection. Ray generation requires taking in image coordinates, transforming them to camera space, generating the ray, and then transforming them to world space. First, I already know the ray's origin in world space (the camera), so I only have to return pos for that. I now have to compute the ray's direction. To do this, I start by converting the given field of view angles to radians. I then compute a vector temp with entries ((2.0 * x - 1.0) * tan(hFovRad / 2.0), (2.0 * y - 1.0) * tan(vFovRad / 2.0), -1.0) where the formulas in the 1st two entries are given and convert the coordinates (x, y) to camera space from the field of view angles hFov/vFov. The third entry of -1 is because we say the sensor center is 1 unit away in camera space (ie. the center of sensor is at (0,0,-1) by convention). This temp vector is normalized and multiplied by a camera-to-world matrix to convert it to world space. The position from before and this vector is then returned as a Ray object. Pixel sampling was implemented by first sampling uniformly from the square region of the pixel and generating a ray from each sample point. Averaging the radiance from each of these points yields the final radiance for that pixel and primitive intersection then determines (for each generated ray) if/where it hits a primitive in the scene (such as a triangle or sphere), returning the closest valid intersection within the ray's valid range.
Explain the triangle intersection algorithm you implemented in your own words.
The triangle intersection algorithm tried to check if a ray (defined by origin and direction) intersected a triangle (defined by vertices 1, 2, 3). Thus, finding an intersection is similar to finding the solution (t, a, b) of where the origin + t*direction = (1-a-b)*vertex1+a*vertex2+b*vertex3. No solution implies no intersection and a t-value less than 0 also implies that no solution (for our purposes) exists. The algorithm I used computes the dot product of one edge (vertex2-vertex1) with the cross product of the ray's direction and another edge (ray x (vertex3-vertex1)). I check if this is nonzero (I return no intersection if <= epsilon to avoid rounding errors) and compute a by using a dot product of the cross product from above and the edge from the ray's origin to vertex 1 (dividing by the determinant). I then compute a cross product of the edge (ray's origin − vertex1) with the 1st edge (vertex2-vertex1) and calculate the dot product of that with the ray direction (dividing by the determinant) to get b. I run the same check as above and then also check if t (the dot product of the 2nd edge and the recent cross product/determinant) is in the acceptable range (not behind camera or after visible part). I then update the variables with what has been computed: the Barycentric coordinates of the intersection point (and the t value).
All of these are the standard dae files implemented using the algorithms above. Nothing appears unusual and the effects of the light can be seen.

Part 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy

Walk through your BVH construction algorithm. Explain the heuristic you chose for picking the splitting point.
My BVH construction algorithm first calculates a bounding box (rectangular box to fully enclose all the primitives) and the number of primitives. If the number of primitives is less than the max_leaf_size, it returns the node (after setting the first and last iterators of the node). Otherwise, it splits the primitives in the bounding box into two categories and recursively calls itself on those two categories to eventually reach the base case (where each bounding box has less than the max_leaf_size number of primitives). Splitting them into two requires a splitting point, so I chose the longest axis of the bounding box and the heuristic was the mean of the centroids of the primitives along that axis. The reason I chose the longest axis was that it would tend to not make unbalanced partitions that weren't useful (reduces the odds of long/thin bounding boxes). The mean was chosen since it can be efficiently computed. After this is computed, one category is the primitives with centroids to the left of the mean and the second category is the primitives with centroids to the right of the mean. As mentioned above, the function is then recursively called on these two categories. In a worst-case degenerate split, it also checks if all the primitives are in a single category and does a median split by count in that case (prevents infinite recursion). It also returns the node after setting its left and right elements to be the results of the recursive calls above.
These images are clearly more complex than the ones in part 1, so they take far longer to render. Using the BVH allows for the ray intersection to be done/checked rapidly, allowing these to be constructed in a reasonable amount of time.

Part 3: Direct Illumination

Walk through both implementations of the direct lighting function.
Direct lighting functions need to compute zero-bounce and one-bounce illumination. Zero-bounce illumination can be computed by checking if a ray from the camera first intersects the scene at a light source. However, with one-bounce, I had to use a more complex procedure. I implemented uniform hemisphere sampling and importance sampling. Uniform hemisphere sampling samples at an intersection point from num_samples directions uniformly over the hemisphere. From each direction, the function casts a ray and sees if it hits a light source. If it does, it multiplies the BRDF value, the radiance along the ray, and the cos of the angle between the ray and the surface normal at that point. It divides that by the sampling pdf (which is 1/2pi in this case since it is uniform over the hemisphere). This is then divided by the num_samples to get the radiance. Importance sampling uses almost the same process, but instead samples directions towards each light source. So, from each light source, a ray is cast towards a sampled point on the light and the same equation from above is used (ie. the one with the BRDF, radiance, cos, pdf) if it is unoccluded. For each area light, this process is repeated num_samples times and averaged (point lights sampled once).

As seen in the images above, increasing the number of light rays from 1 to 64 reduces noise in soft shadows. With 1 light ray, shadows appear very grainy (noticeable black noise on the floor) due to high variance in the estimate (only a single sample). At 4 and 16 rays the noise visibly decreases, and at 64 rays the soft shadows are smooth since the large number of samples has lower variance and has converged.
Compare the results between uniform hemisphere sampling and lighting sampling in a one-paragraph analysis.
As seen in the images above, importance sampling tends to produce significantly less noise than uniform hemisphere sampling with the same number of light rays/samples. Importance sampling directs rays toward light sources, so fewer samples are wasted on directions that contribute no illumination/do not affect the rendering. Uniform hemisphere sampling samples all directions equally, so many rays miss light sources entirely and contribute zero radiance, requiring many more samples to converge (making noisier images as seen on the right column). While this part isn't evident in the images, the computational cost (per sample) for importance sampling is often greater since it needs to go through each light source num_samples times while uniform hemisphere sampling only requires num_samples rays (not dependent on number of lights).

Part 5: Adaptive Sampling

Explain adaptive sampling. Walk through your implementation of the adaptive sampling.
As seen in part 4, some images can be noisy with low sample rates. Increasing the sample rate can help reduce this effect, but increasing the number of rays going through each pixel can be computationally expensive. However, not all parts of the image need an equal amount of 'work' (ie. more uniform areas can be done with a few samples while complex regions may benefit from more samples). Adaptive sampling uses this property by measuring the uncertainty of each pixel value and terminating early on pixels that converge quicker. (eg. continuing on the aforementioned complex regions and terminating earlier on the simple regions). This means that some regions can quickly converge while others are continually sampled, allowing for lower computational work than continually sampling every single pixel.
In my implementation, I defined two variables: s1 (which is the total sum of the luminance values at each sample) and s2 (which is the total sum of the squared luminance values at each sample). n is the number of samples and samplesPerBatch is how often the 'convergence condition' is checked. Thus, after taking the i+1th sample, I check if i is a multiple of samplesPerBatch. If it is, I compute s1/n as the mean of the luminance and (s2-(s1^2)/n)/(n-1) as the (unbiased) variance. This allows me to calculate I= 1.96*sqrt(variance/n) and compare it with maxTolerance * mean. If I is less than or equal to that, I stop sampling at that pixel and save the color value/iteration step (used in rendering the image/sample rate image). That last condition represents convergence since it is checking if the confidence interval (the I) is small relative to the mean radiance of the pixel.
